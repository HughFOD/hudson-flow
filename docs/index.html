Mass-Balance Validation of Streamflow Gauges at the Hudson–Mohawk Confluence

The U.S. Geological Survey measures river flow at gauges across the country. Its data are used for flood forecasting, water management and research. This project checks three neighbouring gauges against each other over four years. The test is a basic principle of chemical engineering: water is conserved, so the flow in must equal the flow out.

Main resultOn a typical day the gauges agree to within 5%. The exception is December 2022 to November 2023, when they disagreed by about 2,600 cubic feet per second, which points to a calibration error at one gauge.

cfs = cubic feet per second, the volume of water flowing past a gauge each second. One cubic foot is about 28 litres, so Green Island's typical 12,775 cfs is roughly 360,000 litres every second.

The idea

Three gauges, one rule

Near Troy, New York, the Mohawk River joins the Hudson. The U.S. Geological Survey measures the flow on each river and again just downstream at Green Island, where they have combined.

So the water passing Green Island should equal what came down the two rivers an hour or two earlier. When it doesn't, either a gauge is wrong or water is getting in or out somewhere nobody measures. Finding out which is the whole project.

What I found

Finding 1

Most of the time, the gauges agree

On a typical day the water adds up to within 5.1% (median gap +5.1%). Hour to hour it is noisier (13.5%), because the dams and locks between the gauges hold water back and release it in pulses. That noise is real river behaviour, not bad data.

Finding 2

My first explanation was wrong

The gap looked seasonal, so I suspected the Erie Canal, which diverts part of the Mohawk around its gauge and returns it to the Hudson above Green Island. I tested that against the canal's real opening and closing dates for 2022–2025.

It doesn't hold up. In cubic feet per second the gap is the same with the canal open (591) as closed (639), and it moved the expected way at only 3 of 5 openings and closings. It only looked seasonal in percent terms, because the river is low in late summer.

Finding 3

The real pattern is a year-long step

From December 2022 to November 2023 the gap jumps to about 2,600 cfs, then drops back almost overnight. Outside that year it is 99 cfs (+0.7%).

Something physical in the river would follow the seasons or the flow. A gap that switches on, stays for a year and switches off looks like a measuring problem: one gauge's calibration drifting, then being corrected.

Next test, in progress. USGS crews measure each river by hand several times a year, and the gauges are calibrated against those visits. Comparing each gauge with the hand measurements during the offset year will show which one drifted.
Finding 4

4.7% of readings needed a quality flag

Most flags are routine: periods USGS marks as estimated, often because of ice, plus stuck or spiking sensors. For most of 2024 Green Island only published estimated values every two hours, and the Mohawk's salt and temperature sensors were stuck or spiking for long stretches. Real data also exposed bugs synthetic data never did, like a download that silently dropped every December 31st.

Finding 5

Forecasting works, modestly, and honestly

Five models predict Green Island's flow ahead of time. Each is trained only on the past and scored on the future, as it would be used. Skill is the share of error removed compared with assuming the flow stays the same.

Boosted trees cut 12-hour forecast error by 20%. The pure mass-balance forecast lost to "no change", because of the calibration offset it can't see. Its 90% prediction ranges were calibrated so that about 90% of real outcomes land inside.

What I learned

Physics beats rulesA sensor drifting out of calibration still reports believable numbers. Only checking it against its neighbours exposes it.
Test your own explanationThe canal story fitted the percentages perfectly and was wrong. Measuring in cfs and checking real dates found the actual cause.
Report what lostThe simple physics forecast lost to "no change". Saying so is more useful than hiding it.

How it's built

DataUSGS Water Data API (15-minute readings and field measurements), Open-Meteo rainfall.
MethodsPython, polars, scikit-learn. Walk-forward testing, leakage checks, conformal prediction intervals, Isolation Forest.
ChecksAutomated tests plant known faults in simulated rivers and confirm every check catches them.
